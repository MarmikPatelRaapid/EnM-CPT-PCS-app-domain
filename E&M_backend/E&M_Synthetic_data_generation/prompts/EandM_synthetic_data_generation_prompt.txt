### **Core Instructions & Guidelines:**


**1. Understand the E/M codes guidelines:**

E/M codes are classified based on facility type, service type, and patient status (new or established). Along with these, the method of selection either total time or the level of Medical Decision Making (MDM) is also an important factor in determining the appropriate code. Together, these elements are used to assign the correct E/M code. The categories and their corresponding details are outlined below for a better understanding.

### Facility Type:
* Facility type refers to the location or care environment in which the service is provided. The facility setting establishes the clinical context of the encounter and often influences the applicable E/M code range.
* Different facility types in E/M are:
*   **`Telemedicine Services`**: Real-time or asynchronous healthcare services delivered remotely using telecommunications technology, including video, audio, or digital portals.
*   **`Hospital Inpatient and Observation Care`**: Evaluation and management services provided to patients admitted to a hospital inpatient bed or placed in outpatient observation status.
*   **`Office or Other Outpatient Services`**: Evaluation and management services provided to patients in outpatient settings, such as physician offices, clinics, or ambulatory care centers.
*   **`Consultations`**: Specialist evaluation and management services requested by another provider to obtain an expert opinion or recommendation regarding a patient's care.
*   **`Emergency Department`**: Evaluation and management services provided to patients in an organized, 24-hour hospital facility designed for immediate or urgent care.
*   **`Critical Care Services`**: Direct, hands-on medical care for patients with life-threatening, highly unstable, or acutely failing organ systems requiring constant clinician attention.
*   **`Nursing Facility Services`**: Evaluation and management services provided to patients residing in nursing facilities, skilled nursing facilities (SNFs), or long-term care facilities.
*   **`Home or Residence Services`**: Evaluation and management services provided to patients in their private home, assisted living facility, or other group residential setting.
*   **`Prolonged Services`**: Additional clinician time spent on patient care beyond the standard evaluation and management service threshold, either with or without direct patient contact.
*   **`Case Management Services`**: Coordination and supervision of care across multiple settings and providers to ensure comprehensive, efficient, and cost-effective patient management.
*   **`Care Plan Oversight Services`**: Non-face-to-face supervision and coordination of care for patients under the care of home health agencies, hospices, or nursing facilities.
*   **`Preventive Medicine Services`**: Services focusing on health promotion, risk-factor assessment, screening, and counseling to prevent illness, disease, or injury.
*   **`Non-Face-to-Face Services`**: Healthcare interactions conducted without physical contact, such as digital, telephone, or electronic portal-based evaluations, consultations, and monitoring.
*   **`Special Evaluation & Management Services`**: Specialized medical examinations, such as those performed for basic life, disability, or work-related insurance eligibility.
*   **`Newborn Care Services`**: Evaluation, stabilization, and ongoing management of healthy or low-risk infants in inpatient or outpatient settings immediately following birth.
*   **`Delivery/Birthing Room`**: Medical attendance, stabilization, and active resuscitation of a newborn immediately following birth in a delivery or birthing room setting.
*   **`Inpatient Neonatal & Pediatric Critical Care`**: High-intensity, life-sustaining medical care provided in inpatient settings to critically ill neonates (28 days or younger) and pediatric patients.
*   **`Cognitive Assessment & Care Plan Services`**: Comprehensive evaluation of cognitive function and the development of a structured, multi-disciplinary care plan for patients with cognitive impairment.
*   **`Care Management Services`**: Non-face-to-face clinical services, such as chronic care or principal care management, designed to support patients with chronic or complex health conditions.
*   **`Psychiatric Collaborative Care Management`**: A structured, team-based care model coordinating behavioral health services between a primary care clinician, a care manager, and a consulting psychiatrist.
*   **`Transitional Care Management Services`**: Care coordination services provided to patients transitioning from an inpatient hospital setting back to a community-based or home setting.
*   **`Advance Care Planning`**: Explanation and structured discussion of advance directives, end-of-life care preferences, and medical decision-making with patients, families, or surrogates.
*   **`General Behavioral Health Integration Care Management`**: Care coordination services provided to patients with behavioral health conditions within a primary care setting under a collaborative model.
*   **`Standby Services`**: Prolonged clinician attendance and readiness to assist a patient, without direct face-to-face contact, at the request of another provider.
*   **`Other E&M Services`**: Evaluation and management services that are not fully described by or classified under any other specific E/M category or CPT code.

### Patient Status:
Patient Status indicates whether the patient is considered new or established. A new patient has not received professional services from the same physician or qualified healthcare professional (or another provider of the same specialty within the same group practice) within the previous three years. An established patient has received such services within that timeframe. Patient status affects the selection of eligible E/M code ranges for many outpatient services.

## E/M Coding Methods

### Time:
* Time can be used as an alternative method to select the appropriate evaluation and management code when the total time spent by the physician or qualified healthcare professional on the date of service is clearly documented. This time includes both face-to-face and non-face-to-face activities directly related to the patient’s care. It is especially useful when counseling, care coordination, or documentation takes more time than the physical examination or when MDM alone does not fully reflect the work performed.

**Key Points:**

* Time-based coding is used instead of MDM when allowed by the E/M code set
* Includes total physician/QHP time on the same date of service
* Covers both in-person and non-face-to-face clinical work
* Must be clearly documented in the medical record
* Only includes provider work directly related to patient care

**Includes:**

* Reviewing medical records and test results
* Patient examination and evaluation
* Counseling and education of patient/family
* Ordering tests, medications, or procedures
* Documentation in the medical record
* Care coordination and communication with other providers
* Independent interpretation of diagnostic results

**Does NOT include:**

* Clinical staff time
* Travel time
* Time spent on separately billable procedures
* Administrative tasks unrelated to patient care

### Medical Decision Making (MDM):

**Medical Decision Making (MDM)** represents the complexity of the provider's clinical judgment during a patient encounter. Under current E/M coding guidelines, MDM is one of the primary factors used to determine the level of many E/M services. It reflects the amount of thought, analysis, risk assessment, and management decisions involved in caring for the patient.

MDM is determined by evaluating **three elements**:

#### 1. Number and Complexity of Problems Addressed

This element assesses the medical issues that were evaluated, treated, or actively managed during the encounter.

Examples include:

* A minor self-limited condition (e.g., common cold)
* A stable chronic illness (e.g., controlled hypertension)
* An acute uncomplicated illness (e.g., uncomplicated urinary tract infection)
* An acute illness with systemic symptoms (e.g., pneumonia with fever)
* Multiple chronic conditions requiring management
* A severe illness posing a threat to life or bodily function

In general, the more complex, numerous, or severe the problems addressed, the higher the MDM level.

---

#### 2. Amount and Complexity of Data Reviewed and Analyzed

This element measures the provider's effort in obtaining, reviewing, interpreting, and discussing patient-related information.
Examples include:

* Reviewing laboratory results
* Reviewing imaging studies
* Reviewing external medical records
* Obtaining history from an independent historian (e.g., family member)
* Ordering diagnostic tests
* Independently interpreting tests
* Discussing patient management with another physician or healthcare professional

The greater the amount and complexity of data reviewed or analyzed, the higher the MDM level.

---

#### 3. Risk of Complications and/or Morbidity or Mortality of Patient Management

This element evaluates the risk associated with the provider's treatment decisions and management plan.

Examples include:

* Conservative management with minimal risk
* Prescription drug management
* Decisions regarding minor surgery
* Decisions regarding major surgery
* Hospitalization decisions
* Escalation of care
* Management of life-threatening conditions

The higher the potential risk to the patient resulting from the condition or management decisions, the higher the MDM level.

---

### MDM Levels

#### Straightforward MDM

Typically involves:

* One minor or self-limited problem
* Minimal or no data review
* Minimal risk

Example:

* Follow-up for a resolved viral infection requiring no treatment changes.

#### Low MDM

Typically involves:

* One stable chronic illness or uncomplicated acute illness
* Limited data review
* Low-risk management decisions

Example:

* Follow-up for controlled hypertension with medication continuation.

#### Moderate MDM

Typically involves:

* Multiple chronic illnesses
* Acute illness with systemic symptoms
* Moderate amount of data review
* Prescription drug management or other moderate-risk decisions

Example:

* Evaluation of diabetes and hypertension requiring medication adjustment and review of laboratory results.

#### High MDM

Typically involves:

* Severe or life-threatening conditions
* Extensive data review and analysis
* High-risk management decisions

Example:

* Management of sepsis, acute respiratory failure, or a patient requiring intensive monitoring and hospitalization.

# Synthetic EHR Document Generation Guidelines

## 1. Objective

Generate realistic, clinically plausible Electronic Health Record (EHR) documents in plain text format. The document should resemble authentic healthcare documentation while ensuring that all patient, provider, facility, and encounter information is entirely fictional.

The document type, structure, and content must be appropriate for the clinical scenario and code(s) being represented.

## 2. General Document Requirements

### 2.1 Text Format Only

* Output must be generated as plain text.
* Do not use tables, HTML, XML, JSON, markdown tables, or structured data formats.
* Maintain a professional healthcare documentation style.

### 2.2 Dynamic Header

Every document must begin with a realistic EHR-style header containing fictional metadata.
Example fields:
Hospital Name: <Fictional Facility Name>
Department: <Relevant Department>
Patient Name: <Fictional Patient Name>
MRN: <Fictional Medical Record Number>
DOB: <Fictional>
Age: <Fictional>
Sex: <Fictional>

Document Type: <Dynamic Based on Note Type>
Provider: <Fictional Provider>

The exact header fields may vary depending on document type and also keep it dynamic and change templates for each provider.

### 2.3 Dynamic Footer

Every document must end with a realistic EHR-style footer.

Possible footer elements:
Document Generated Date/Time
Provider Signature
Electronic Signature Statement
Facility Name

Example:

Electronically Signed By:
Dr. John Smith, MD

Date Signed: 01/15/2025 14:35

End of Report

Footer content should vary naturally between documents.

## 3. Clinical Authenticity Requirements

### 3.1 Realistic Clinical Narrative

* Use clinically plausible language.
* Ensure consistency between diagnoses, procedures, findings, and impressions.
* Avoid contradictory information.
* Include realistic medical terminology appropriate to the specialty.

### 3.2 Complete Documentation

Each document should contain sufficient supporting evidence to justify:

* Diagnoses
* Procedures
* Findings
* Medical decision making
* Clinical outcomes

### 3.3 Internal Consistency

All sections must align logically. Some examples are:

H&P: Header → Reason for Encounter → History → Exam → Assessment → Plan
Consultation Note: Header → Consultation Request → History → Exam → Impression → Recommendations
Progress Note: Header → Interval History → Findings → Assessment → Plan
Discharge Summary: 	Header → Admission Information → Hospital Course → Diagnoses → Discharge Plan → Follow-up


## 4. Document-Type Specific Templates

The generated document structure must adapt based on the clinical document category.

# History and Physical (H&P) Note

Use when the code represents a comprehensive initial evaluation, inpatient admission, or pre-operative assessment.

Required Sections: 

* Chief Complaint (CC)
* History of Present Illness (HPI)
* Past Medical, Surgical, Family, and Social History (PFSH)
* Review of Systems (ROS)
* Physical Examination (PE)
* Assessment and Plan (A&P)

Optional Sections:

* Current Medications
* Allergies (with reactions)
* Vital Signs
* Laboratory and Diagnostic Data (results reviewed)
* Code Status / Advance Directives

Additional Notes:

* HPI should present a chronological narrative of the patient’s presenting symptoms.
* Review of Systems (ROS) must be clinically consistent with the HPI and Physical Examination to avoid documentation contradictions.
* Physical Examination should detail objective, system-by-system findings.
* Assessment and Plan should clearly list active diagnoses, differential diagnoses, and specific management steps (e.g., medications ordered, consults requested, or further testing).

Examples:

* Inpatient Admission H&P
* Pre-operative / Pre-surgical Clearance H&P
* Comprehensive Outpatient Consultation
* New Patient Preventive Medicine Evaluation

---

# Consultation Note

Use when a physician’s opinion or advice is requested by another clinician for a specific patient.

Required Sections:

* Requesting Provider / Referral Source
* Reason for Consultation (clinical question to be addressed)
* History of Present Illness (HPI)
* Physical Examination (PE)
* Assessment (comprehensive impression)
* Recommendations (specific diagnostic or therapeutic plan)

Optional Sections:

* Past Medical, Surgical, Family, and Social History (PFSH)
* Review of Systems (ROS)
* Diagnostic/Laboratory Data Reviewed
* Time spent counseling/coordinating care

Additional Notes:

* Must explicitly identify the requesting clinician and the specific consult question.
* Recommendations must be clearly delineated so they can be communicated back to the requesting provider.

Examples:

* Cardiology Consultation (e.g., pre-operative risk assessment)
* Infectious Disease Consultation (e.g., refractory bacteremia)
* Endocrinology Consultation (e.g., uncontrolled diabetes management)

---

# Progress Note

Use for routine follow-up encounters (outpatient or daily inpatient rounds) tracking ongoing patient care.

Required Sections:

* Subjective (interval history, patient concerns, symptom updates)
* Objective (vital signs, physical exam, reviewed laboratory or imaging data)
* Assessment (status of active problems/diagnoses)
* Plan (interventions, medication changes, diagnostic workup, follow-up)

Optional Sections:

* Review of Systems (ROS)
* Cumulative lab/imaging trends
* Preventive services reviewed

Additional Notes:

* Commonly structured as a SOAP (Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan) note.
* Should focus on interval changes since the last encounter or previous hospital day.

Examples:

* Daily Inpatient Progress Note
* Chronic Disease Outpatient Follow-up (e.g., Hypertension, Chronic Kidney Disease)
* Post-operative Day 2 Progress Note

---

# Emergency Department Note

Use for acute evaluations, diagnoses, and treatments performed in an emergency care setting.

Required Sections:

* Chief Complaint (CC)
* History of Present Illness (HPI)
* Physical Examination (focused or comprehensive based on acuity)
* ED Course (treatments administered, medication response, reassessments)
* Disposition (e.g., discharge, admit to inpatient, transfer, AMA)

Optional Sections:

* Past Medical/Surgical History
* Diagnostic Results (EKG interpretation, point-of-care ultrasound, labs)
* Consultations obtained in the ED

Additional Notes:

* Must document frequent reassessments, particularly changes in patient status following acute therapeutic interventions (e.g., post-bronchodilator exam).
* Focus is placed on ruling out life-threatening differentials.

Examples:

* Acute Chest Pain Evaluation
* Closed Head Injury / Concussion Assessment
* Laceration Repair and Tetanus Prophylaxis

---

# Observation Note

Use when a patient is placed in outpatient observation status for monitoring, testing, or therapy prior to deciding on discharge or inpatient admission.

Required Sections:

* Reason for Observation Status
* Interval History / Current Status
* Physical Examination
* Diagnostic Results / Monitoring Data
* Plan of Care / Final Disposition

Optional Sections:

* Specialist Consultation Summaries
* Social factors affecting safe discharge

Additional Notes:

* Documentation must reflect the ongoing clinical necessity of hospital-level monitoring without meeting full inpatient criteria.
* Usually covers a 24- to 48-hour time window.

Examples:

* Post-Procedural Observation (e.g., post-cardiac catheterization monitoring)
* Chest Pain Protocol Observation
* Intractable Vomiting / Dehydration IV Hydration Protocol

---

# Discharge Summary

Use at the conclusion of an inpatient or extended observation stay to summarize the entire hospitalization.

Required Sections:

* Date of Admission and Date of Discharge
* Principal Diagnosis & Key Secondary Diagnoses
* Summary of Hospital Course (treatments, diagnostic findings, procedures)
* Discharge Physical Examination
* Discharge Medications (with changes from admission explicitly noted)
* Follow-up Instructions (appointments, pending tests, precautions)

Optional Sections:

* Condition at Discharge (e.g., stable, improved)
* Pending Lab or Culture Results

Additional Notes:

* Must clearly bridge inpatient care to outpatient care, serving as the primary communication tool for the primary care physician.
* Active, discontinued, and newly prescribed medications must be clearly reconciled.

Examples:

* Inpatient Medical Discharge Summary (e.g., post-COPD exacerbation)
* Post-Surgical Discharge Summary
* Pediatric Hospitalization Discharge Summary

---

# Nursing Facility Note

Use for encounters with patients residing in skilled nursing facilities (SNFs), intermediate care facilities, or long-term care facilities.

Required Sections:

* Reason for Encounter / Interval History
* Physical Examination
* Assessment of Functional/Cognitive Status
* Medication Review / Care Plan Reconciliation
* Plan of Care (specific to facility-level care)

Optional Sections:

* Inputs from nursing or therapy staff
* Dietary/Nutritional Review
* Wound care tracking

Additional Notes:

# Critical Care Note

Use when a provider delivers direct medical care for a critically ill or critically injured patient with acute organ system failure or high risk of deterioration.

Required Sections:

* Nature of Life-Threatening/Unstable Condition (clinical status)
* Total Critical Care Time (expressed in minutes; must exclude procedural time)
* Active Life Support Interventions (e.g., mechanical ventilation, vasopressor titration)
* Physical Examination
* Complex Medical Decision-Making and Treatment Plan

Optional Sections:

* Family Discussion Details (concerning surrogate decision-making)
* Review of continuous physiological monitoring data

Additional Notes:

* **Total time spent on critical care must be explicitly documented (e.g., "75 minutes spent in critical care, excluding procedural time").**
* Time spent on separately billable procedures (e.g., intubation, central line placement) cannot be counted toward critical care time.

Examples:

* Sepsis with Septic Shock Daily Critical Care Management
* Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS) Ventilator Management
* Acute Myocardial Infarction with Cardiogenic Shock Emergency Stabilization

## 7. E/M Specific Rules

**1. Ensure Narrative Cohesion and Clinical Realism:**
All parts of the note must be medically consistent. The `Subjective` and `Objective` must logically lead to the `Assessment and plan`. The `Postoperative Diagnosis` should reflect the `Findings`. Use standard medical terminology and abbreviations correctly. Medical details should be documented with sufficient clinical context and realistic detail, reflecting the structure and content of authentic medical notes, including the procedure performed, approach, key findings, devices used (if applicable), and outcome.

**2. Maintain Medical Necessity:**
Ensure the volume of documentation matches the clinical severity of the presenting problem. Do not pad the note with exhaustive, unrelated multi-system reviews of systems or comprehensive physical exams for minor, localized complaints (e.g., do not perform a full neurological exam for an uncomplicated ankle sprain).

**3. Implicit vs. Explicit Coding Cues:**
Do not use coding terminology or justify the E/M level within the clinical text. For example, do not write 'This represents moderate complexity decision-making' or 'A prescription was written to satisfy Level 4 billing.' The clinical actions must speak for themselves.

**4. Provide Specific Time Parameters (If Testing Time-Based Coding):**
If generating a time-based note, include a specific statement detailing the total time spent on the date of the encounter, breaking down face-to-face and non-face-to-face work (e.g., reviewing records, documenting, counseling) to align with AMA guidelines.

**5. Enforce Strict Internal Consistency:**
Ensure clinical consistency across all sections. Symptoms described in the HPI must be evaluated in the Physical Exam and addressed in the Assessment and Plan. Avoid contradictory findings unless clinically explained (e.g., a resolved symptom).

## 8. Synthetic and Secure
All patient details, including names, dates, medical record numbers, and locations, must be entirely fictional. No real patient information should ever be used.
